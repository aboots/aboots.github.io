How well does an AI explain what it sees?

An AI system recognizes bird species in photos that have been damaged by noise, blur, fog, compression and similar effects. It explains each decision by pointing to regions of the photo and showing similar-looking regions from birds it saw during training.

You will see 16 short screens. Each shows one damaged bird photo and two different explanations (A and B) of the AI's decision. You pick which explanation is better (or "No clear difference" if you really cannot tell them apart). There are no right or wrong answers, and you do not need to know anything about birds.

  • Takes about 8–10 minutes. Please use a laptop or desktop screen.
  • Participation is voluntary and anonymous. We do not collect your name, email or any identifying information.
  • You can stop at any time by closing the page; nothing is sent until you press Submit at the end.
  • Your answers are used only for research on explainable AI and may be published in aggregate.

How to read an explanation

Each explanation is one row like the one below.

  • Left: an undamaged copy of the same photo, with small coloured, numbered boxes marking the regions the AI relied on when it looked at the damaged photo. (We draw the boxes on the undamaged copy so you can see which parts of the bird they cover.) Boxes with the same colour and number belong together.
  • Right: five training examples, numbered 1 to 5. Example k has the same colour and number as group k on the left; its own boxes show the region the AI thinks looks like group k.
  • The numbers rank the evidence by importance. Number 1 contributed most to the AI's score for the bird species it chose, number 2 the next most, and so on down to number 5. Each number applies to its whole group of boxes and to its training example.

When you compare two explanations, check two things:

  1. The order: is the most important evidence on the bird? Give the most weight to the top of the ranking (number 1, then 2, …). Do these boxes cover meaningful parts of the bird (head, beak, eye, wing, tail, body markings), or background, noise or damage? Poor evidence near the top of the ranking is worse than poor evidence near the bottom.
  2. The matches: does each part match the same kind of part? For each number, compare the boxed region on the left with the boxed region in the training example with the same number. A good match shows the same kind of part and looks alike (head with head, wing with wing). A poor match pairs different parts (for example, a wing with a head) or a bird part with background.

The better explanation is the one whose most important evidence is on meaningful parts of the bird and whose matches make sense.
